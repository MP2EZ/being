**Document scope:** Internal compliance artifact. Regulator-facing only. Not for public distribution.
**Document type:** Lawful-basis assessment record (GDPR Art. 6(1)(d), Art. 9(2)(c))
**Processing activity:** `crisis_detected` event — Supabase `analytics_events` table
**Version:** 1.6
**Date:** 2026-06-03 (amended 2026-09-13, 2026-10-23, 2026-10-24)
**Author:** Palouse Labs LLC
**Related work item:** INFRA-214 T5; amended under DEBUG-541, which discharges the §5 payload-expansion review clause, under DEBUG-608 (§1 purpose wording), and under DEBUG-850 (identifiability correction; §3 re-run), and under MAINT-865 (INFRA-851 session IP minimisation reviewed), and under INFRA-861 (the row no longer carries an account or session identifier; §2–§5 re-run; retention shortened to 90 days)

## 1. Purpose

When Being detects a PHQ-9 total score ≥20, a non-zero PHQ-9 Q9 (self-harm ideation) response, or a GAD-7 total score ≥15, it records a single `crisis_detected` event to the first-party Supabase `analytics_events` table. The purpose is twofold: (a) operational safety monitoring — to allow the founder to verify that crisis-resource interventions (988 prompt, crisis-resources screen) are being surfaced at the correct thresholds; and (b) aggregate pattern observation — to detect any systematic failure in the crisis-detection path across the user base.

**Corrected (DEBUG-608).** The paragraph above previously named a "safety-plan display" among the interventions this telemetry verifies. Being has no safety-plan feature and no shipped build ever had one; the intervention surfaced is the crisis-resources screen. The purpose, the payload and the §3 balancing are unaffected.

This processing occurs without analytics consent and is not suppressible by the universal opt-out, because its lawful basis is vital interests rather than consent or legitimate interests.

## 2. Necessity Assessment

**Why consent is not the appropriate basis:** Crisis-detection telemetry must fire at the moment of threshold detection, before any consent dialog could be meaningfully presented. A user in a crisis state should not be required to grant analytics consent before crisis resources are surfaced or before the triggering event is logged. Conditioning safety-path telemetry on consent would also allow a user to inadvertently prevent logging by opting out before a later crisis event, defeating the operational safety purpose.

**Why legitimate interests is not the appropriate basis:** The processing involves special-category health data (mental-health condition data derived from PHQ-9/GAD-7 responses) per GDPR Art. 9. Art. 9(1) prohibits processing of special-category data; the legitimate-interests basis in Art. 6(1)(f) does not on its own override Art. 9(1). A specific Art. 9 derogation is required. Art. 9(2)(c) (vital interests where the data subject is or may be physically incapable of giving consent) is the appropriate derogation, applied in conjunction with Art. 6(1)(d).

**Minimum necessary payload — corrected (DEBUG-409), widened to five fields (DEBUG-541).** The event payload is limited to `trigger_type` (category label — one of `phq9_suicidal_ideation`, `phq9_severe_score`, `phq9_moderate_severe_score`, `gad7_severe_score`), `severity_bucket` (e.g. `high` / `critical`), `intervention_surfaced` (boolean), `assessment_type` (`PHQ-9` / `GAD-7`), and — since DEBUG-541 — `detected_on`, the UTC calendar day on which the threshold was crossed, as `'YYYY-MM-DD'`. No raw score, no Q9 numeric value, no time of day, and no device identifier is included.

**`detected_on` is day-precision by decision, not by convenience.** A full-precision ISO-8601 timestamp was considered and REJECTED. The row is pseudonymous rather than anonymous — it carries the persistent `user_id` described below — so a sub-day timestamp would have added three disclosures the four coarse labels do not: `created_at − detected_on` would be device clock skew (a fingerprinting and cross-principal linkage key), the exact time of day of a crisis is a behavioural-health inference of a different sensitivity class from "a threshold was crossed", and the same delta would disclose the length of a user's offline periods. Every consumer of this field is day-granular, so nothing operational is lost. The consequence is accepted explicitly: this forfeits deriving delivery latency from `created_at − detected_on`, and the field must not later be widened to recover it — that would be a fresh minimisation question, not an extension of this one.

⚠️ **This paragraph previously read "and no persistent session token is included", and described `session_id` as "daily-rotated … does not persist across calendar days". Both were inaccurate, and the correction matters to §3's balancing.** Two facts, verified against the code:

1. **Every transmitted row carries a persistent user-level identifier.** `analytics_events.user_id` is `NOT NULL REFERENCES users(id)` (`supabase/migrations/20260523000000_base_schema.sql`), populated from the INFRA-260 `signInAnonymously` principal — i.e. `auth.uid()`, the same identity used for optional encrypted backups. It persists across sessions and across calendar days. It is first-party, RLS-isolated, non-enumerable, and never linked to an email, profile or sign-in identity (Being has no sign-in UI), and it is not itself wellness data — but it is **persistent**, and describing it as absent was wrong.
2. **`session_id` did not actually rotate within a process — corrected in code by INFRA-568.** It was minted once in the `SupabaseService` constructor and never re-generated, so a session spanning midnight UTC carried the previous day's prefix and a long-lived process produced one "session" spanning days; the daily-rotation claim held only across app restarts. Since INFRA-568 it is regenerated lazily when the embedded UTC date is stale **or** more than 30 minutes have elapsed since it was last written, so it now links only events within one engagement. **This does not make the original claim true**, and it was never the load-bearing safeguard: fact 1 above is why. The date component remains UTC-derived by design, matching `created_at` and the operator views' `DATE_TRUNC('day', …)`.

Neither fact was previously falsified in practice, because until DEBUG-409 the delivery path was dead code and the table had received exactly one row in its lifetime. The identifiability conclusion in §3 should be read against the corrected description above: re-identification is managed by severity-bucketing, first-party RLS isolation, the non-enumerability of `auth.uid()` and the absence of any name, email or sign-in identity — **not** by the absence of a persistent identifier, and not by any lack of quasi-identifiers (corrected DEBUG-850, below).

**Corrected (DEBUG-850, 2026-10-23) — the identifier joins network attributes.** The paragraph above previously listed "the absence of quasi-identifiers" among the re-identification controls. That was not true of the stored data. `user_id` is the Supabase Auth principal, and Supabase Auth keeps, per session, the IP address and user agent of the latest connection (`auth.sessions.ip` / `user_agent`, rewritten on every token refresh; 144 of 144 hosted sessions carried an IP on 2026-10-22, per MAINT-847). For the controller, `analytics_events.user_id` → `auth.sessions` is one join, so a crisis-detection row is linkable to a network address and a coarse device string (app build number plus OS kernel version, verified 2026-10-23). Supabase request logs also hold the insert request's IP for 1 day on the current plan. The transmitted payload is unchanged by this correction — the five fields are as enumerated above — and the IP address and user agent are **not** necessary to the safety purpose: they are incidental to transport and authentication, stored by the auth layer for every principal, and their minimisation is tracked as INFRA-851 (**updated MAINT-865, 2026-10-24:** INFRA-851 is live, and both columns are cleared hourly, see §5). Non-identifiability therefore rests on first-party storage, RLS isolation, the absence of any name, email or sign-in identity, no third-party sharing, and the fact that no Being code reads `auth.sessions.ip` or `user_agent` — not on the payload lacking quasi-identifiers. The §3 balance is re-run for this correction (§3, DEBUG-850 paragraph).

**Superseded in part (INFRA-861, 2026-10-24) — the row no longer carries an account or session identifier.** Facts 1 and 2 above and the DEBUG-850 paragraph describe rows recorded before INFRA-861; none of those rows remains, because the INFRA-861 migration deleted every pre-change crisis row. Since that migration (`supabase/migrations/20261024000000_infra861_crisis_delink.sql`):

- **The table enforces the de-link for every role.** A BEFORE INSERT trigger sets `user_id` and `session_id` to NULL, truncates `created_at` to the UTC day, and rebuilds `properties` from the five allowlisted keys, dropping every other key; a value outside a key's fixed domain becomes `'unknown'` (or null for `intervention_surfaced`), and a `detected_on` that is not a `YYYY-MM-DD` string is dropped. CHECK constraints hold that shape for every role, service_role and UPDATE included, so a later trigger edit or an operator write cannot reintroduce an identifier.
- **Delivery uses no identity.** The app sends crisis rows through a dedicated lane with the project's public key and no user token. The anon role holds INSERT on `event_type` and `properties` only and no SELECT, under a policy that admits only crisis rows. Authenticated crisis inserts are closed, and the crisis path no longer creates the random account ID, so `auth.users.created_at` no longer marks a first detection.
- **The domains are wider than the enumeration above.** The server admits the trigger types and assessment types every current emitter sends, including the journal keyword scan (`journal_text_match`, `voice_journal`); anything else is stored as `'unknown'`. No new kind of field is added.

**Residual linkage after INFRA-861 — recorded, not argued away.** The row is not anonymous, and this assessment does not call it so. Whether it meets the state-law deidentified-data definitions is a counsel question (§5); under GDPR it is treated as pseudonymous personal data, and data-subject requests are handled under Art. 11.

1. **Request logs.** Supabase request logs keep the connecting IP address and a timestamp for 1 day on the current plan. The request is an insert on `analytics_events` by the anon role, and only crisis rows are inserted by that role, so for that day a log line can show that a given IP address sent a crisis event. No Being code reads these logs, and no operator procedure queries them for these inserts. A plan change that lengthens log retention reopens this item.
2. **Small cohort.** With the founder as the only user today, and a small, known group of testers next, a day-level row can be attributed by inference. The views apply no k-anonymity suppression, by design (a safety monitor must not hide the first crisis). The control is the public commitment in privacy-policy §3 not to try to work out whose an event is.
3. **Co-timed writes.** Day truncation leaves only same-day co-occurrence with the identified operational rows the same foreground handler may send. Heap order and transaction ids still give the controller sub-day ordering against adjacent writes; the row id must never become time-ordered.
4. **Offline gap.** The `created_at − detected_on` gap still discloses, at day resolution, that the device was offline across that interval (§3, DEBUG-541).

These remain the minimum fields needed to fulfil the operational safety purpose. The payload was unchanged by DEBUG-409; it was widened by one field by DEBUG-541, which re-ran the §3 balancing rather than amending this enumeration alone — see the DEBUG-541 paragraph in §3.

## 3. Balancing Test

Where Art. 6(1)(d) is applied alongside Art. 9(2)(c), a full legitimate-interests balancing test is not strictly required, because the vital-interests basis is precisely intended to override the ordinary consent requirement in life-safety contexts. The following is recorded for completeness.

**Nature of the data:** The payload encodes a derived category (crisis threshold crossed) rather than the underlying responses. A severity bucket is less sensitive than a raw PHQ-9 score. The `session_id` is bounded-lifetime (UTC day boundary or 30 minutes idle, INFRA-568) and so links only events within one engagement — but it is **not** what prevents persistent linkage, because `user_id` is persistent and is on every row. Non-identifiability rests on pseudonymity and isolation, per §2 and safeguard 2 below — not on the payload lacking quasi-identifiers (corrected DEBUG-850). The processing does not reveal the user's identity to any third party.

**Reasonable expectations:** A user who completes a PHQ-9 or GAD-7 in a mental-wellness app that has disclosed crisis-detection and safety-resource features would reasonably expect that threshold-crossing events are monitored by the app operator to ensure the safety path works correctly.

**Consequences for the data subject:** The payload itself carries no name, contact detail or answer, and nothing usable to re-contact or disadvantage the data subject. **Corrected (DEBUG-850):** this sentence previously read that the payload carries no information usable to identify the data subject; the row is linkable by the controller to the latest IP address and user agent of the data subject's session (§2). The first-party Supabase table is governed by Row-Level Security; cross-user queries are not possible at the application layer. The data is not shared with any third party. The primary consequence for the data subject is that crisis-resource failures are more likely to be detected and corrected — in the data subject's interest.

**Counterweight:** The data subject's privacy interest in not having a derived crisis-threshold indicator recorded to a server is real. It is outweighed in this context by the safety interest above and the minimal content of the payload in first-party, RLS-isolated storage, further reduced by the safeguards in §4. (This sentence previously relied on "the minimal identifiability of the payload"; the identifiability is not minimal — see the DEBUG-850 re-run below.)

**Re-run for the fifth field — `detected_on` (DEBUG-541, 2026-09-13).** §5 commits this assessment to review if the payload expands. It has expanded by one field, so the four sub-tests above are re-run here rather than the enumeration in §2 being amended on its own.

*Nature of the data.* `detected_on` adds no new **kind** of sensitive fact. The sensitive fact — that a crisis threshold was crossed — is already on the row via `trigger_type` and `severity_bucket`. What is added is the calendar placement of an already-disclosed event. The sensitivity class that day precision structurally cannot encode is time-of-day, and that was rejected for exactly this reason (§2).

*Reasonable expectations.* Strengthened rather than weakened. A user who understands that Being monitors whether its crisis path fired correctly would expect the record to reflect when the threshold was actually crossed, not when their handset regained signal. The prior `NOW()`-only behaviour is the one that is harder to square with the disclosed purpose.

*Consequences for the data subject.* One genuinely new consequence: the gap between `detected_on` and `created_at` discloses, at day resolution, that the device was offline or backgrounded across that interval. This is accepted as a residual. It is bounded to day granularity, confined to first-party RLS-isolated storage under a non-enumerable `auth.uid()`, shared with no third party, and marginal against the identifiers already on the row. It does not move re-identification from "not realistically achievable" (see `dpia-sensitive-wellness-data.md` §6.1). That DPIA conclusion was itself withdrawn by DEBUG-850; see the re-run below.

*Counterweight.* The purpose served is the **same** purpose already declared in §1(b) — detecting systematic failure in the crisis-detection path — not a new one. Without event time, that monitoring is actively wrong rather than merely coarse: a batch of stale events flushed on reconnection reads as a same-day spike, and the trailing baseline the alerter uses is seeded from the wrong days. This is proportionate widening in service of the existing vital-interests basis, not purpose creep.

*Conclusion.* The §3 balance is reaffirmed under the five-field payload. Two controls are load-bearing to that conclusion and are recorded as safeguard 1: the value is clamped server-side so it can never exceed the ingest date, and it is a date with no clock component.

**Re-run for the identifiability correction (DEBUG-850, 2026-10-23).** Nothing transmitted changed, so the §5 payload-expansion clause is not engaged. The §2 premise that re-identification was limited by an absence of quasi-identifiers was wrong, though, and §3's conclusion leaned on it, so the four sub-tests are re-run rather than §2 being amended alone.

*Nature of the data.* Unchanged in kind: no new field and no new sensitive fact. What changes is the description of linkage. A row is linkable, by the controller, to the latest connection's IP address and a coarse user agent. `analytics_events.created_at` is the unrounded server arrival time, so on an online device it falls within seconds of the detection: the stored row carries a time of day even though `detected_on` does not, and the day-precision reasoning in §2 governs the transmitted field only. On an install with no backup or purchase the crisis flush is what mints the identity, so the identity's creation time and first-session IP also mark the first detection, even after later refreshes rewrite the session row.

*Reasonable expectations.* The privacy policy previously told users the event was free of personal information and contained nothing that identified them. That is corrected in privacy-policy v1.25, so expectations are now set by an accurate disclosure rather than weighing against the processing on a false premise.

*Consequences for the data subject.* The new consequence is re-identification risk if `auth.sessions` and `analytics_events` were exposed together: crisis status identifiable by IP address. The controls are first-party storage, RLS, the auth schema not being exposed through the API, founder-only access, no code path reading the session columns, and the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule backstop. Being itself holds no name or contact dataset to join an IP address to, and has no re-contact path.

*Counterweight.* The purpose and basis are unchanged, and the IP address and user agent are not used for the purpose. The proportionate response is to minimise them (INFRA-851) and to bound identities orphaned by a reinstall (INFRA-848), not to stop safety monitoring.

*Conclusion.* The §3 balance is reaffirmed under the corrected description, on the condition that INFRA-851 and INFRA-848 land before the first external tester. The vital-interests basis is unchanged. **Updated (MAINT-865, 2026-10-24):** the INFRA-851 half of that condition is discharged (§5). The INFRA-848 half is not: INFRA-848 landed as a census only, and deleting orphaned identities is INFRA-852.

**Re-run for the de-link (INFRA-861, 2026-10-24).** What is transmitted and stored changed (the identifiers are removed, `created_at` is coarsened, retention is shortened), so the four sub-tests are re-run.

*Nature of the data.* The sensitive fact is unchanged: a crisis threshold was crossed, with a category, a severity bucket, whether support was shown, an assessment type and a detection day. What is removed is the link to a person: no `user_id`, no `session_id`, no identity created for the event, and a `created_at` with no time of day. The day-precision reasoning in §2 now governs `created_at` as well as `detected_on`.

*Reasonable expectations.* Strengthened. Privacy-policy v1.33 tells users the event is stored without the random account ID or any session identifier, that the provider's request logs record the sending IP address for up to a day, and that the event cannot be deleted on request for that reason.

*Consequences for the data subject.* The one-join route to a network address through `auth.sessions` is gone for crisis rows, and identities orphaned by a reinstall (INFRA-848 / INFRA-852) no longer bear on them. Two consequences are new. First, the data subject can no longer have their crisis rows deleted, because no one can tell which rows are theirs; this is bounded by a 90-day retention (safeguard 8) and by deleting every pre-change row rather than de-linking it, so no row collected under the old promise that deletion removes it survives. Second, anyone holding the public project key can now insert crisis rows, which could corrupt the safety signal this processing exists to produce; the controls are the per-statement cap of 50 rows, coercion of every value to a fixed domain, and a daily ceiling with an operator alert. None of them collects an identifier. The residual linkages in §2 remain.

*Counterweight.* The purpose and the vital-interests basis are unchanged, and nothing operational used the identifiers: the views and the alerter read day-level counts only.

*Conclusion.* The §3 balance is reaffirmed and strengthened. The DEBUG-850 condition on INFRA-848 no longer bears on crisis rows, which carry no identity.

## 4. Safeguards

1. **Payload minimization.** The emitter writes an explicit allow-list of five bucketed/categorical fields (four since INFRA-214; `detected_on` added by DEBUG-541); it never spreads the detection object (which holds the raw `triggerValue`). The Supabase sanitizer additionally severity-buckets any clinically-named numeric as a backstop. Two constraints on the fifth field are part of this safeguard rather than incidental to it: it is a UTC **date** with no clock component, and it is **clamped server-side** so a value later than the ingest date is discarded in favour of the ingest date. `detected_on` derives from the device clock, which is untrusted; the clamp is what stops a skewed or manipulated handset placing a detection at a date that never occurred, and thereby corrupting the safety signal this processing exists to produce. A value that cannot be parsed, or that falls outside the accepted range, falls back to the ingest date — it is never dropped, and never rounded to the boundary (which would manufacture a detection on a day that had none). **Extended (INFRA-861).** The allow-list is now also enforced by the server: a trigger rebuilds `properties` from the five keys and drops every other key, and a CHECK refuses any other key for every role (`supabase/migrations/20261024000000_infra861_crisis_delink.sql`). The client allow-list is no longer the only control.
2. **Pseudonymous identifiers — corrected (DEBUG-409).** This safeguard previously read: *"Daily-rotating session_id. The anonymous token is generated at app launch and replaced after a calendar-day boundary. **No persistent user-level identifier is stored or transmitted.**"* The final sentence was false. Each row carries `user_id` — the persistent INFRA-260 `auth.uid()` anonymous-session principal (`analytics_events.user_id` is `NOT NULL REFERENCES users(id)`) — plus a `session_id` whose rotation was per-process rather than per-calendar-day. INFRA-568 fixed the rotation itself — it is now regenerated when the embedded UTC date is stale or after 30 minutes idle — but that changes nothing about this safeguard's basis, which was never the token. The accurate safeguard is **pseudonymity and isolation, not absence**: `auth.uid()` is a first-party, RLS-isolated, non-enumerable UUID never linked to an email, profile or sign-in identity, shared with no third party, and carrying no wellness data of its own. (DB CHECK constraint enforces the `session_YYYY-MM-DD_…` format.) **Corrected again (DEBUG-850).** `auth.uid()` is never linked to an email, profile or sign-in identity, but Supabase Auth stores it with the latest connection's IP address and user agent (`auth.sessions`), so it is pseudonymous rather than unlinkable — see §2. See §2 for why this went unfalsified until now. **Superseded (INFRA-861).** Crisis rows now carry no `user_id` and no `session_id`; a trigger and CHECK constraints enforce both for every role (§2). The safeguard is the **absence of an account or session identifier**, held by the table, not pseudonymity. It is not anonymity: see the residual linkage in §2.
3. **Durable, lossless capture — scope corrected (DEBUG-409).** The event is enqueued durably at fire-time (independent of network/userId provisioning) and reconciled to the anonymous user row when available, so a first-run/offline crisis is recorded rather than silently dropped. Undeliverable events are retained for retry and surfaced to the local security log. ⚠️ Until DEBUG-409 this described **capture only, never delivery**: a client was constructed solely for users who had opened Profile → Cloud Backup, so for everyone else the queue accumulated and never flushed. Since DEBUG-409 the crisis lane provisions its own client on the first queued event, independently of `cloud_sync` consent, so the safeguard now describes the running system. **Changed (INFRA-861).** The queue is no longer reconciled to any user row: the lane sends without an identity and never creates one. A pre-change build's crisis insert is now refused by the server; its events stay in the durable queue and are delivered through the new lane after the app is updated.
4. **First-party storage only.** `analytics_events` is in Being's own Supabase project. No third party receives crisis-detection telemetry.
5. **Row-Level Security.** RLS isolates rows by an opaque identifier; no cross-user query is possible at the application layer. Technical basis: `docs/security/supabase-rls-verification.md`. **Changed (INFRA-861).** Crisis rows carry no identifier for RLS to isolate. No client role can read them: anon holds no SELECT, and the authenticated SELECT policy matches `user_id = auth.uid()`, which a NULL `user_id` never satisfies.
6. **Local footprint minimization — corrected (MAINT-334).** Versions of this assessment before 2026-08-06 recorded the opposite safeguard here: that a separate, mandatory on-device crisis-intervention audit (`logCrisisIntervention`) recorded every detection independently of this telemetry. DEBUG-305 removed that write, and the claim has been false since. It is corrected rather than deleted because the accurate position is a **stronger** safeguard for the balancing test in §3, not a weaker one: the app retains no durable local record of crisis-intervention *content*. Residual records on pre-fix installs are deleted at launch by `legacyPlaintextRecordSweeper`.

**Corrected a second time (DEBUG-381), and the correction is about completeness, not content.** This safeguard previously read "the app no longer retains any durable local crisis-intervention record" and named `crisis_analytics_queue` as "the only local artifact that remains". Both were **false when written**. Two further AsyncStorage keys described crisis-tier records at that time — `audit_log_<epoch>` (closed by DEBUG-355) and `storage_metadata_index` (closed by DEBUG-381) — and neither was enumerated here or in the DPIA. This is the third consecutive version of this safeguard to assert local completeness and be falsified, each time discovered by enumerating the store after erasure while testing the previous fix.

The safeguard is therefore restated in a checkable form. **The accurate claim is about content, not artifacts:** no durable local record holds crisis *content* — no score, no Q9 value, no free text. Several local artifacts do record that a crisis-tier record *existed* and when; all are now swept on account erasure, and all are enumerated positively in `dpia-sensitive-wellness-data.md` §3 rather than asserted away here. That table, not this sentence, is the authority — a negative completeness claim in prose has now been wrong three times.

**Effect on the §3 balancing test: none.** The balancing conclusion rests on the minimal identifiability of the **transmitted** payload — five bucketed categorical fields (four at the time this correction was written; `detected_on` added by DEBUG-541) plus the pseudonymous identifiers described in §2 as corrected by DEBUG-409 (`user_id` = the RLS-isolated `auth.uid()` principal, and a per-process `session_id`) — which no local write affects. The local-footprint safeguard is corroborating, not load-bearing. Had it been load-bearing, three successive falsifications would have required re-running the balance rather than amending the text.

The completeness interest the original safeguard was reaching for is served by safeguard 3's durable enqueue-and-reconcile, not by a second local copy. `@being/supabase/crisis_analytics_queue` remains the pending-upload buffer described in safeguard 3: the four bucketed fields, plus the enqueue timestamp from which the transmitted `detected_on` is derived at flush time; cleared on successful flush, swept on account erasure (`SWEPT_EXACT_KEYS`). See `dpia-sensitive-wellness-data.md` §3 (positive key enumeration), §7 controls 14–16 and 18–19, and §9 v1.8 / v2.0.

**DEBUG-413 (2026-08-14) — one bounded exception to "cleared on successful flush".** Buffer entries enqueued strictly before `PRE_FIX_CRISIS_BACKLOG_CUTOFF_MS` are now discarded at load **without** being transmitted. This is a one-time correction, not a change of policy, and it does not touch the transmitted payload — the four-field minimum enumerated above was unchanged by DEBUG-413, so the §3 balancing and the review clause below were not engaged **by that item**. They were engaged later, by DEBUG-541, which is the branch the next paragraph anticipated; see the DEBUG-541 re-run in §3.

Why it was necessary. Until DEBUG-409 landed, `flushCrisisAnalytics` early-returned for every device that had not opened Profile → Cloud Backup, so essentially the entire history of enqueued crisis events was still buffered locally and undelivered. The flush projection drops `enqueued_at` and `analytics_events.created_at` defaults to `NOW()`, so delivering that buffer would have stamped months-old events with the delivery date — producing a false spike in the operator views, a false-positive page from the INFRA-219 alerter, and a retention clock anchored from the wrong date. Delivering inaccurate data was not the more protective option: it would have degraded the vital-interest monitoring the processing exists to serve, while adding nothing a data subject benefits from.

What was weighed. The discarded entries are undelivered, PII-free bucketed counters from a pre-launch population (founder devices plus a small TestFlight cohort), dominated by synthetic QA triggering rather than genuine distress. No data-subject right is diminished: nothing is being withheld from a subject, and erasure already swept this key. The alternative — carrying the true event time through to the server — would have widened the transmitted payload to a fifth field and is therefore the branch that *would* have engaged the review clause; it was rejected here on that basis among others. **That branch was subsequently taken (DEBUG-541, 2026-09-13), and the review clause it names was discharged rather than bypassed:** the §3 balancing was re-run for `detected_on` at day precision, safeguard 1 was extended with the clamp and the no-clock-component constraint, and §5 records the outcome. This paragraph is left standing as written because it correctly describes the decision made at the time.

Residual, recorded rather than absorbed: the underlying `NOW()` behaviour is unchanged, so a device that is offline for an extended period still delivers stale events stamped with the delivery date. Bounded, and tracked separately. **Closed by DEBUG-541 (2026-09-13)** — that item is the "tracked separately" referred to here. Rows carrying `detected_on` are now attributed to the detection day; rows without it (emitted by clients predating the change) continue to fall back to the delivery date, and that mixed population is permanent rather than a transition window, so the operator views and the alert body label the fallback share rather than hiding it.

**Second correction (DEBUG-355, 2026-08-08).** The paragraph above previously ended its first sentence with "…so the data subject's local exposure is now nil rather than an unencrypted plaintext record on the device." That phrase was **false when written** and has been struck. Two further local crisis-path writes survived DEBUG-305 and were still live: `critical_log_<epoch>` to SecureStore for every ERROR-level or CRISIS-category log entry, and `audit_log_<epoch>` to AsyncStorage for every `crisis_tier` storage operation. The first was written in cleartext in every real configuration and was **unerasable by construction** — SecureStore does not enumerate, so account deletion could not reach it and no sweeper could be built for it.

This matters here specifically, and not only in the DPIA, because the sentence was doing work: §3's balancing test weighs the data subject's privacy interest against the safety interest, and "local exposure is nil" was an input to that weighing. An overstated safeguard makes a balancing test unsound even where the conclusion survives — and the conclusion does survive, because the §3 balance rests on the minimal identifiability of the *transmitted* payload, which these local writes never affected.

The corrected position: the writes are removed at source (`critical_log_*`) and brought under the erasure sweep (`audit_log_*`) as of DEBUG-355. Prospectively, local exposure on this path is nil. Retrospectively, `critical_log_*` records on installs predating that change are irreducible and are recorded as an **accepted residual** in `dpia-sensitive-wellness-data.md` §8 — bounded, never transmitted, OS-keystore-protected, and containing only sanitized log fields rather than scores, responses, or identifiers. See also `dpia-sensitive-wellness-data.md` §7 controls 17–18 and §9 v1.9.
7. **Transparency.** The privacy policy (§3 Safety Features; §5.2 Analytics) discloses that crisis-detection events are recorded to first-party storage under a vital-interests basis without analytics consent. Since privacy-policy v1.25 (DEBUG-850) it also discloses that the event is linked to the random account ID and that Supabase stores the latest connection's IP address and user agent against that identifier (§4.1, §5.1). Since privacy-policy v1.30 (MAINT-865) it says both are deleted within a day of each connection. Earlier versions described the event as free of personal information, which was inaccurate. **Since privacy-policy v1.33 (INFRA-861)** §3 says the event is stored without the random account ID or any session identifier, that the provider's request logs record the sending IP address for up to a day, that Being will not try to work out whose an event is, and that the event cannot be deleted on request for that reason; §7.4 says pre-change events were deleted.
8. **Storage limitation — bounded and enforced (DEBUG-340).** `crisis_detected` rows are retained for **3 years** from the recorded date and then deleted, matching the period published in privacy-policy §7.2. Enforcement is a scheduled database job (`analytics-retention-prune`, daily), not a manual process. Every other `analytics_events` row is deleted at 90 days by the same job. This safeguard is recorded here because this assessment previously stated no retention period at all: the function existed but was never scheduled, so actual server-side retention was **indefinite** — which would have undermined the proportionality argument in §3, since an unbounded window cannot be "limited to the minimum required for the operational safety purpose" however small the payload. The 3-year figure is a business choice, not a regulatory floor or ceiling: no statute fixes a retention period for crisis records held by a consumer wellness app that is not a healthcare provider (see `regulatory-applicability.md`). It is justified by the safety-monitoring-continuity purpose in §1 and is the period already published to users. **Changed (INFRA-861, founder decision 2026-10-24): 90 days from receipt.** The 3-year justification included evidence in legal proceedings, which a row tied to no one cannot serve, and nothing operational reads further back than 90 days (the alerter's baseline is 7 days; the views floor detection days at 90). `cleanup_old_analytics()` becomes a single 90-day rule for every event type. Retention runs from receipt, so a row can describe a detection up to about 90 days older than its arrival. Privacy-policy v1.33 §7.3 publishes the period.
